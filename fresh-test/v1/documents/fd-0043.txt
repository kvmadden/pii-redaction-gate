Freida B. Addison is an 81-year-old woman admitted to the Heart and Vascular Center of Elm Crossing after syncope at home. Her daughter-in-law, Hope Yang, saw her slump in a kitchen chair and called for help. Her grandson, Price Yang, arrived during the evaluation and remained for teaching. She had been lightheaded for two days. She denied chest pain, a clearly recalled palpitation, focal weakness, and melena.

History includes hypertension, hypothyroidism, and symptomatic bradycardia that had not yet been treated with a device. She does not have diabetes. Because the serum sodium was low, a morning cortisol was obtained and returned at 18.4 mcg/dL, with corticotropin in the reference range. That result argues against Addison disease, and she has never been treated for adrenal insufficiency. Home medicines were levothyroxine 88 mcg by mouth each morning, amlodipine 5 mg by mouth daily, and aspirin 81 mg by mouth daily. She has no known drug allergy.

Arrival vitals were blood pressure 96/60 mmHg, pulse 38 beats per minute, respiratory rate 16, and oxygen saturation 97 percent on room air. She was pale, alert, and oriented. The lungs were clear, there was no murmur, and there was no edema. The electrocardiogram showed complete heart block with a slow escape rhythm.

Laboratory studies: sodium 131 mmol/L, potassium 4.4 mmol/L, creatinine 0.9 mg/dL, thyroid stimulating hormone 2.2 mIU/L, and troponin I 0.01 ng/mL repeated once without a rise. The hyponatremia was attributed to poor intake around the bradycardia, not to adrenal failure.

Dr. Rémi de la Croix implanted a dual-chamber pacemaker on the 4th of March under local anesthesia with light sedation. Thresholds were acceptable and there was no pneumothorax on the post-procedure radiograph. The generator serial number is HVC-2044-881. She was observed overnight on telemetry. The lower rate was programmed to 60 beats per minute. By the next morning the blood pressure was 128/74 mmHg, the paced rate was 72 beats per minute, and she walked the corridor without dizziness or chest pain.

Hope Yang practiced checking the incision and reviewed warning symptoms: fever, increasing pocket swelling, persistent hiccups, twitching of the chest wall, or another syncopal spell. Price Yang confirmed he could stay in the house for the first two nights. The left arm on the implant side should not be raised above the shoulder for six weeks, and the dressing should stay dry for five days.

Anticoagulation was considered because of a slow atrial rate on the monitor, but there was no atrial fibrillation on telemetry and no prior stroke, so aspirin alone was continued. She may climb the stairs at home. She should not drive for one week, a point Price Yang repeated back to the nurse. A sling was advised for the first day only. Wound check is not required unless the site reddens or drains.

Discharge medicines are unchanged aside from acetaminophen 650 mg by mouth every six hours as needed for soreness: levothyroxine 88 mcg each morning, amlodipine 5 mg daily, and aspirin 81 mg daily. No anticoagulant was added. Device interrogation with Dr. Rémi de la Croix is planned in two weeks at the Heart and Vascular Center of Elm Crossing, earlier if the warning symptoms appear.
